Finding a Meeting Time That Doesn't Wake Someone Up at 4 AM

Scheduling across two timezones is manageable; scheduling across four or five, where no single time works well for everyone, turns into a genuine optimization problem. There's rarely a perfect answer, but there is a reliably better process than guessing and hoping nobody complains.

How to Find Overlapping Working Hours

  1. Open the World Clock and add every participant's city or timezone.
  2. Compare each participant's current local time side by side.
  3. Identify the window where the most participants' standard working hours genuinely overlap.
  4. Double-check the proposed time again closer to the actual meeting date, since daylight saving shifts can move the correct time on one side.

A Reliable Process for Multi-Timezone Scheduling

The single most effective habit for multi-timezone scheduling is converting every participant's standard working hours into one shared reference timezone before trying to find an overlap, rather than juggling several different local times in your head simultaneously. List each person's typical 9-to-5 (or whatever their actual working hours are) converted into, say, UTC, then look for the specific UTC window where the largest number of participants' working hours genuinely overlap. This single-reference-point approach removes most of the mental juggling that causes scheduling mistakes, and it scales cleanly regardless of how many timezones are actually involved — three, five, or eight participants all get converted into the same shared reference frame, making the overlap immediately visible rather than requiring separate pairwise comparisons between every possible pair of participants.

Real-World Scenarios

  • "My team spans California, London, and India — what time works?" Convert all three to UTC first: California (UTC-8 typically), London (UTC+0), India (UTC+5:30). The narrow overlap tends to land in California's late morning, London's late afternoon/evening, and India's late night — a genuinely tight window worth confirming with a live world clock rather than estimating.
  • "Our weekly meeting keeps landing at an awkward time for one region." Check whether the meeting was set up with timezone-aware scheduling or a fixed offset — a fixed offset is very likely the culprit if the awkwardness appeared gradually rather than from the start.
  • "We have team members in regions with zero working-hours overlap." Rotating who attends outside normal hours, or shifting non-critical updates to asynchronous formats, are both more sustainable than repeatedly asking the same person to accommodate everyone else.
  • "I want to propose a meeting time without guessing." Add every participant's city to a live world clock, check each one's actual current local time, and propose a specific time only after confirming it genuinely falls within reasonable hours for everyone involved.

Why This Problem Has Gotten More Common, Not Less

Remote and distributed work arrangements have made genuinely wide-timezone-spread teams far more common than they were when most organizations operated out of a single office or a small cluster of nearby offices in similar timezones. This shift means the multi-timezone scheduling challenge, once a relatively niche concern mostly affecting international sales or global operations teams, now affects a meaningfully larger share of the working population directly, on a weekly or even daily basis. The tools available for managing this have improved considerably alongside the trend — timezone-aware calendar software, world clock widgets built directly into many operating systems, and scheduling assistants that automatically suggest overlap windows have all become standard rather than niche — but the underlying challenge itself, finding time that genuinely works across meaningfully different local working hours, remains a real constraint that better tools alleviate rather than eliminate entirely, since there's still a hard physical limit on how much overlap exists between regions on opposite sides of the globe regardless of how good the scheduling software gets.

When There's No Good Overlap at All

For teams spanning a wide enough spread of timezones — say, one participant in California and another in India, roughly 12-13 hours apart depending on daylight saving — a genuine, comfortable working-hours overlap may simply not exist. In this situation, the honest options are limited: someone attends slightly outside their normal working hours, the meeting gets kept deliberately short and tightly focused to minimize the inconvenience, or the interaction shifts to an asynchronous format that doesn't require everyone present simultaneously at all. Pretending a comfortable overlap exists when it genuinely doesn't tends to produce chronic frustration for whoever ends up bearing the inconvenient time slot repeatedly, which is exactly the situation the next section's rotation approach is specifically designed to address fairly.

Rotating the Inconvenient Slot Fairly

ApproachHow It Works
Fixed inconvenient timeSame person or region always attends outside normal hours — simple but unfair over time
Rotating inconvenient timeThe early/late slot moves between regions on a set schedule, spreading the burden
Alternating meeting existenceMeeting alternates between two different times, so each region gets a convenient slot roughly half the time

For genuinely recurring meetings where no single time works comfortably for everyone, explicitly rotating who bears the inconvenient slot is widely regarded as the fairer long-term approach, since it distributes the burden rather than consistently asking the same region or individual to accommodate everyone else indefinitely. This requires slightly more scheduling overhead than a single fixed time, but teams that adopt this practice consistently report it feels noticeably more equitable than the alternative of one region silently absorbing the inconvenience meeting after meeting without acknowledgment.

Why Recurring Meetings Drift Over Time

A meeting time calculated as correct today can silently become wrong months later if it was scheduled using a fixed time difference rather than genuine timezone-aware calendar scheduling, precisely because different regions shift their clocks for daylight saving on different calendar dates. A meeting locked in as "9 AM my time, 7 PM yours" during one part of the year can require a different actual local time on one side the moment that region's daylight saving transition happens while the other region's clocks stay put, silently shifting the effective meeting time by an hour for whoever's region moved. Using calendar software that stores events with proper timezone metadata (rather than a manually calculated fixed offset) avoids this drift entirely, since the software recalculates the correct local time for each participant automatically as daylight saving transitions occur on either end throughout the year. This drift is particularly sneaky precisely because it happens gradually and without any obvious announcement — the meeting invite still says the same nominal time, and only careful attention (or a confused participant showing up an hour off) reveals that the underlying calculation quietly stopped matching reality the moment one region's clocks moved.

When to Just Go Asynchronous Instead

Not every interaction genuinely requires everyone live and present at the same moment, and recognizing this can eliminate a scheduling headache entirely rather than solving it. Status updates, non-urgent decisions, and information sharing frequently work perfectly well asynchronously — recorded video updates, written summaries, or collaborative documents that people engage with on their own local schedule — reserving genuinely synchronous meeting time specifically for discussions that actually require real-time back-and-forth interaction. Teams that default to synchronous meetings for everything, regardless of whether real-time interaction is actually necessary, tend to accumulate exactly the kind of chronic timezone scheduling friction this entire topic addresses, much of which disappears once a meaningful portion of routine coordination moves to asynchronous formats instead.

Common Mistakes

  • Comparing timezones pairwise instead of using a shared reference point. Converting everyone to one common reference (like UTC) first makes finding a genuine overlap across many participants far more manageable than juggling several separate local times in your head simultaneously.
  • Scheduling a recurring meeting with a fixed time difference. As covered above, this silently drifts incorrect once either region's daylight saving transition occurs — use timezone-aware calendar scheduling instead, which automatically recalculates as needed.
  • Always asking the same person or region to accommodate an inconvenient time. Rotating the inconvenient slot, where a genuine overlap doesn't exist, distributes the burden more fairly across the whole team over time.
  • Defaulting to a synchronous meeting when asynchronous communication would genuinely work. Not every update or decision requires everyone live simultaneously — moving routine coordination asynchronous can eliminate scheduling friction entirely for a meaningful share of team interactions.
  • Failing to reconfirm a recurring meeting's correctness after a daylight saving transition. Even with good intentions, a quick reconfirmation around known daylight saving change dates catches drift before it causes anyone to miss a meeting.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find a meeting time across three or more timezones?

Convert each participant's working hours to a single common reference timezone, then find the window where the most participants' hours genuinely overlap, accepting a perfect overlap isn't always possible.

How should teams handle timezones with no overlap at all?

Common approaches include rotating the inconvenient slot fairly, scheduling slightly outside standard hours for the more flexible group, or shifting to asynchronous communication instead.

Why do meeting times shift unexpectedly for remote teams?

This usually happens when a meeting was scheduled with a fixed time difference rather than timezone-aware scheduling, and one region's daylight saving transition has thrown off the calculation.

Is there a single fair meeting time for teams spanning many timezones?

Generally no — with enough spread, any fixed time will inconvenience someone. Many teams instead rotate the inconvenient slot to keep the burden balanced over time.

Conclusion

There's no perfect solution to scheduling across widely spread timezones, but there is a genuinely better process: convert everyone to a shared reference point, look honestly at whether a real overlap exists, and when it doesn't, rotate the burden fairly or move the interaction asynchronous rather than repeatedly asking the same person to absorb the inconvenience. Combined with timezone-aware calendar tools that automatically handle daylight saving drift, this process turns a genuinely frustrating recurring problem into something manageable, predictable, and fair for every participant regardless of where in the world they happen to be working from.
