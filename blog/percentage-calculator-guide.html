Every Percentage Calculation You'll Actually Need, Explained

Percentages show up constantly — discounts, grades, tips, growth rates, statistics — yet the handful of distinct calculations hiding behind the word "percentage" trip people up more often than the concept deserves. Knowing which specific formula a given question actually calls for is most of the battle, and the rest comes down to a few well-known traps that are easy to avoid once you know they exist.

Finding What Percent One Number Is of Another

This is the classic "what percent" question: what percent is 45 of 60? The formula is (part ÷ whole) × 100. Dividing 45 by 60 gives 0.75, and multiplying by 100 converts that decimal into the percentage, 75%. This calculation shows up constantly in contexts like test scores (points earned out of points possible), completion rates (tasks done out of total tasks), and any situation comparing a portion against its total.

Finding a Percentage of a Number

The reverse-feeling but equally common question: what is 20% of 150? Here the formula is number × (percentage ÷ 100), or equivalently, convert the percentage to a decimal and multiply directly: 150 × 0.20 = 30. This calculation is the one behind discount pricing (20% off $150 means subtracting $30), tipping (15% of a $60 bill), and tax calculations (adding a percentage-based tax onto a base price).

Percentage Increase and Decrease

ScenarioFormula
Percentage increase((new − old) ÷ old) × 100
Percentage decrease((old − new) ÷ old) × 100

Both formulas are really the same calculation — the difference between two values, divided by the original (starting) value, expressed as a percentage. If a price rises from $80 to $100, the increase is ((100−80)÷80)×100 = 25%. If it then falls back from $100 to $80, the decrease is ((100−80)÷100)×100 = 20% — not 25%, since the base value used in the division is different in each direction. This asymmetry is one of the most common sources of confusion in percentage change calculations, and understanding why it happens (the denominator changes depending on which direction you're measuring) resolves most of the confusion at its root.

Working Backward: Finding the Original Number

Sometimes the question runs in reverse: a price after a 20% discount is $80 — what was the original price? Since $80 represents 80% of the original (100% minus the 20% discount), the calculation is $80 ÷ 0.80 = $100. This reverse calculation trips people up more than the forward version, since the intuitive but incorrect approach — simply adding 20% back onto $80 — gives $96, not the correct $100, precisely because 20% of $80 isn't the same amount as 20% of the original $100. Working backward always requires dividing by the remaining percentage (as a decimal) rather than adding the percentage back onto the reduced figure.

The instinct to simply add the percentage back is understandable, since it mirrors how the forward calculation felt — subtract a percentage to go down, so add it back to go up, right? The flaw is that the percentage in the reverse direction needs to be calculated against a different, larger base than the one used going forward, and that base is exactly the unknown value you're trying to solve for. This is the same underlying reason percentage increases and decreases aren't symmetric, covered in more detail below — every percentage calculation is relative to a specific starting point, and confusing which starting point applies in which direction is the single most common source of percentage-related calculation errors people run into in everyday situations like sale pricing, tax calculations, or reversing a reported growth rate back to find an original figure.

Real-World Scenarios

  • "I scored 42 out of 50 on a test — what percentage is that?" Use the "what percent" formula: (42 ÷ 50) × 100 = 84%.
  • "An item is 30% off and originally cost $80 — what's the sale price?" Find 30% of $80 (which is $24), then subtract: $80 − $24 = $56.
  • "A company's revenue grew from $2 million to $2.5 million — what's the growth rate?" Use the percentage increase formula: (($2.5M − $2M) ÷ $2M) × 100 = 25%.
  • "After a 15% discount, an item costs $85 — what was the original price?" Divide by the remaining percentage: $85 ÷ 0.85 = $100, working backward correctly rather than adding 15% onto $85.

Percentage Points vs. Percent Change

A distinction genuinely worth knowing, especially in contexts like interest rates, statistics, or election polling: if a rate moves from 10% to 15%, that's an increase of 5 percentage points, but a percent change of 50% relative to the original 10% figure — ((15−10)÷10)×100 = 50%. These two descriptions of the same underlying change sound similar but communicate meaningfully different things, and conflating them is a common source of confusion or, in some cases, deliberately misleading framing in reporting or marketing. A "5 percentage point increase" is a precise, unambiguous statement about the absolute change in the rate itself; a "50% increase" describes the same event relative to where it started, and can sound far more dramatic for small starting percentages even when describing an identical underlying shift. Recognizing which framing a source is using — and being able to convert between them yourself — is a small but genuinely useful piece of numeracy for reading anything involving rates or percentages critically.

Why You Can't Just Average Percentages

A genuinely common and consequential mistake: assuming a 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease returns you to where you started. It doesn't. Starting at 100, a 50% increase brings you to 150. A subsequent 50% decrease is calculated on the new base of 150, not the original 100 — so it removes 75, bringing the final value to 75, not back to 100. This happens because each percentage change is calculated relative to whatever the current value is at that moment, not relative to some fixed original baseline, and it's exactly why sequential percentage changes (like stacked discounts, or investment returns over multiple periods) can't be simply added or averaged together to find the combined effect — each step needs to be calculated relative to the result of the previous step.

Percentages of Percentages: Compound Situations

Real situations often chain multiple percentage operations together, and each step needs to be calculated against the result of the previous step, not the original starting value. A common example: an item is marked down 20%, and then an additional 10% is taken off at checkout. The second 10% discount applies to the already-reduced price, not the original price — so a $100 item first drops to $80 (after 20% off), then the 10% additional discount removes $8 (10% of $80, not $10, which would be 10% of the original $100), landing at a final price of $72. This produces a combined discount of 28%, not the 30% someone might assume from simply adding 20% and 10% together. This same sequential-calculation principle applies to compound interest, stacked fees, multi-year growth rates, and any other situation involving more than one percentage change applied in sequence — always calculate each step against the current running total, never against the original starting figure, unless a problem explicitly specifies otherwise.

Where Percentages Show Up Most in Everyday Life

  • Shopping and discounts. Sale percentages, cashback offers, and price comparisons all rely on the "percentage of" and reverse calculations covered above.
  • Grades and test scores. Converting raw scores into percentages uses the "what percent" formula, and understanding weighted averages across multiple assessments often requires combining several percentage calculations correctly rather than simply averaging them.
  • Financial statements and business reporting. Growth rates, margin percentages, and year-over-year comparisons all use the percentage change formula, and the percentage-points-vs-percent-change distinction matters considerably for accurately interpreting business and economic reporting.
  • Tips, taxes, and fees. Adding a percentage-based amount onto a base price is one of the most frequent everyday percentage calculations, whether at a restaurant, checkout, or when estimating total cost including tax.
  • Statistics and survey results. Interpreting poll results, survey response rates, and statistical comparisons correctly depends on understanding exactly which percentage calculation and which base value a given figure is relative to.

Common Mistakes

  • Adding a discount percentage back onto the discounted price to find the original. As covered above, this requires dividing by the remaining percentage, not adding the discount amount back.
  • Assuming symmetric percentage changes cancel out. A percentage increase followed by an equal percentage decrease doesn't return to the starting value, since the base changes between the two calculations.
  • Confusing "percent of" with "percent change." These are genuinely different calculations answering different questions — check which one a specific problem actually requires before applying a formula.
  • Averaging percentages from different-sized bases as if they were equivalent. A 10% change on a small base and a 10% change on a large base represent very different absolute amounts, even though the percentage figures match.
  • Confusing percentage points with percent change. A move from 10% to 15% is both a 5-percentage-point increase and a 50% relative increase — these describe the same event differently, and mixing them up leads to genuinely different, and sometimes misleading, conclusions about the size of a change.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate what percent one number is of another?

Divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100. To find what percent 45 is of 60: (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%.

What's the formula for percentage increase or decrease?

Percentage change equals (new value minus old value) divided by old value, multiplied by 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.

Why can't you average two percentages directly?

Averaging percentages ignores the different base sizes each was calculated from. A 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease doesn't return to the original value, since the second percentage uses a different base.

How is a percentage of a number calculated?

Multiply the number by the percentage expressed as a decimal. To find 20% of 150: 150 × 0.20 = 30.

Conclusion

Percentage calculations aren't complicated individually, but recognizing which of the several distinct formulas a given question actually requires is where most confusion happens. Once you can identify whether a problem is asking for a percentage of a number, what percent one number is of another, a percentage change, or a reversed calculation, applying the correct formula becomes straightforward — and understanding the difference between percentage points and percent change, along with why sequential percentage changes don't simply cancel out, rounds out the handful of genuinely useful mental habits that make percentages far less confusing than they initially seem, whether the context is a shopping discount, a business report, or a statistics class.
