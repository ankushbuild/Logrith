How to Compress a PDF for Email or Upload Without Sending It to a Server

Gmail rejects attachments over 25MB. Outlook caps at 20MB. Countless job portals, government forms, and application systems enforce their own strict PDF size limits. Most people solve this by uploading their file to a compression website — but that means handing a resume, contract, or financial document to a third-party server before you've even confirmed the compression worked. Here's how to shrink a PDF entirely inside your own browser instead, and how that approach compares to iLovePDF, SmallPDF, and Adobe's online compressor.

Why Server-Based Compression Is a Real Privacy Tradeoff

The most widely used PDF compressors — iLovePDF, SmallPDF, Adobe's online tool, and dozens of similar sites — all work the same fundamental way: you upload your file to their servers, their infrastructure processes it, and you download the result. This is a completely reasonable approach for a birthday invitation or a public flyer. It's a materially different question when the file is a resume with your home address and phone number, a signed contract, a bank statement, or a medical record — documents where you'd reasonably prefer not to introduce a third party into the chain of custody at all, even one with a good privacy policy.

Most of these services are genuinely trustworthy and delete uploaded files after processing, as their privacy policies state. The distinction this guide is making isn't that they're untrustworthy — it's that browser-based processing removes the question entirely, rather than resolving it through a policy you have to trust. If your PDF is never transmitted anywhere, there's no server log, no temporary storage window, and no policy to rely on, because there was never a hand-off in the first place.

How Browser-Based PDF Compression Actually Works

Unlike direct PDF-structure optimization (which requires deep, format-specific manipulation of the PDF's internal object streams — the kind of processing typically done server-side with specialized libraries), a practical way to compress a PDF entirely within a browser is to render each page as an image, compress that image, and reassemble the compressed images into a new PDF. This is exactly what Logrith's PDF Compressor does: PDF.js renders each page onto an in-memory canvas, that canvas is exported as a JPEG at your chosen quality level, and pdf-lib rebuilds a new PDF document from the compressed page images.

This approach is particularly effective for PDFs that are already mostly images to begin with — scanned documents, photo-heavy reports, or PDFs exported from a scanner app — since the bulk of their file size already comes from image data, which compresses well using this method. It's less dramatically effective on PDFs that are almost entirely native text (a plain text-based invoice, for example), since converting efficiently-encoded text into a rendered image can occasionally increase file size rather than reduce it, a genuine limitation worth understanding rather than glossing over.

Real-World Scenarios: When Each Approach Makes Sense

  • "I need to email a scanned contract that's 40MB." This is the strongest case for page-image compression — scanned documents are almost entirely image data, so even Medium quality typically shrinks a scan-heavy PDF dramatically, often by 70% or more.
  • "My resume PDF is only 2MB but the portal wants under 500KB." A text-based resume PDF with a photo or logo may not shrink as dramatically through page-image compression, since the underlying text was already efficiently encoded. In this case, check whether the size is coming from an embedded high-resolution photo, and consider compressing that image separately before generating the PDF, in addition to running the PDF compressor.
  • "I have a 200-page report full of charts and photos." Expect longer processing time given the page count, but also expect a meaningful size reduction, since chart and photo-heavy pages compress well with this method. Use High quality if chart legibility matters.
  • "I just need this under Gmail's limit, nothing fancy." Medium quality is the sensible default — it balances noticeable size reduction against acceptable visual quality for the vast majority of everyday documents.

Single-File Processing vs. Batch Compression

It's worth being upfront about a genuine limitation: Logrith's PDF Compressor currently handles one file at a time, while iLovePDF and SmallPDF both offer some form of batch processing on their free tiers (with iLovePDF's being more generous). If you routinely need to compress dozens of PDFs at once — processing a folder of scanned invoices, for example — a batch-capable tool will save meaningfully more time than compressing files one by one, even accounting for the privacy tradeoff of uploading them.

For the more common case of compressing a handful of documents as they come up — a single resume before applying to a job, one contract before emailing it, an occasional scanned form — the lack of batch processing is a minor inconvenience rather than a real obstacle, and the privacy benefit of never uploading the file remains fully intact regardless of how many files you process one at a time.

Manual Alternatives to Online Compression

If you have access to desktop software, Adobe Acrobat Pro, Preview (on macOS), or LibreOffice's PDF export can all reduce PDF size locally without any online tool at all — genuinely private since nothing touches a browser or server, but requiring installed software most casual users don't have on hand. For anyone without desktop PDF software, a browser-based compressor is the closest equivalent in terms of keeping the file local.

Common Mistakes When Compressing PDFs

  • Compressing an already-compressed PDF repeatedly. Each re-compression pass can compound quality loss on image-heavy pages, similar to repeatedly re-saving a JPEG. Keep an original master file if you might need to re-compress at a different quality later.
  • Using maximum compression on text-critical documents. Contracts, legal documents, and anything where fine text needs to remain perfectly legible should use High quality rather than Low, even though the file size reduction will be smaller.
  • Not checking the result before sending. Especially at Low quality, always open the compressed file and check a few pages before relying on it for something important.
  • Trying to compress a password-protected PDF. Remove any password protection first, since the compression process needs to read and re-render each page.
  • Expecting text-based PDFs to shrink dramatically. If your PDF is mostly native text rather than scanned images, size reduction from this method will be modest — the bulk of the file size in a photo-heavy or scanned PDF comes from images, and that's where this compression method has the most impact.

Privacy, Security, and Limits

The entire compression process — rendering pages, adjusting image quality, and reassembling the PDF — runs using PDF.js and pdf-lib, both JavaScript libraries executing directly in your browser. No file upload occurs at any point, no server log is created, and there's no account or session tied to your usage. Because there's no network round-trip involved, processing speed depends only on your device's own processing power and the document's page count, rather than upload bandwidth or a queue on someone else's servers.

The realistic limit worth knowing: very large or many-page PDFs will take correspondingly longer to process, since each page is individually rendered and re-compressed by your browser rather than a dedicated server. For typical documents under 50 pages, this remains fast; for very large scanned documents (hundreds of pages), expect noticeably more processing time than a server-based tool with dedicated compute resources.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I compress a PDF to fit Gmail's attachment limit?

Gmail caps attachments at 25MB. Use Medium or Low compression quality, which re-renders each page at a lower image quality, typically shrinking scanned or image-heavy PDFs well below that limit.

Is it safe to compress a PDF with personal or financial information online?

Only if the tool processes the file entirely in your browser. Many popular compressors upload your file to a server first. Logrith's PDF Compressor never does — everything runs locally using PDF.js and pdf-lib.

Why did my compressed PDF get bigger instead of smaller?

This can happen if the original PDF was already highly optimized or mostly text-based, since re-rendering text as an image can sometimes produce a larger file than efficiently-encoded original text.

Does compressing a PDF reduce text sharpness?

With page-image-based compression, yes, to some degree, since each page becomes a rendered image rather than live text. Higher quality settings keep text edges sharper at a larger file size.

Can I compress a password-protected PDF?

You'll need to remove the password first, since compression requires reading and re-rendering each page, which isn't possible on an encrypted file without the password.

What's the difference between compressing a PDF and reducing its page count?

Compression reduces file size of existing pages by lowering image quality. Reducing page count means removing pages entirely, a separate operation using a PDF page remover tool.
