Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between APA, MLA, and Chicago?

APA is common in social sciences, MLA in humanities and literature, and Chicago in history and other disciplines. Each has distinct formatting rules for authors, dates, and titles.

Do I need the exact publication date?

Yes, an accurate publication year is an important part of a correctly formatted citation in all three styles.

What if I don't have a URL?

Leave the URL field blank — the citation will be formatted as a standard print/publisher source instead of an online source.

When Each Citation Style Is Used

  • APA (American Psychological Association) — most common in psychology, education, and other social sciences. Uses author-date format.
  • MLA (Modern Language Association) — most common in humanities, literature, and language studies. Emphasizes author and page number.
  • Chicago — common in history and some other fields, with two distinct sub-styles (notes-bibliography and author-date), this tool generates the bibliography-style format.

Key Formatting Differences

While all three styles cite the same underlying source information, they format it differently. APA places the publication year immediately after the author's name in parentheses. MLA places the publisher and year toward the end of the citation, with less emphasis on the exact date for many source types. Chicago's bibliography style is similar to MLA but with its own specific punctuation and ordering conventions. This tool handles these formatting differences automatically based on the fields you provide.

Why Citations Matter

Proper citation gives credit to original sources, allows readers to locate and verify the material you referenced, and is a fundamental requirement of academic integrity. Different institutions, publications, and disciplines require different citation styles, which is why having quick access to all three formats from the same source details saves time when you're not sure which style a particular assignment or publication requires.
