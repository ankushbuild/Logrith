Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for simple interest?

Simple Interest = (Principal × Rate × Time) ÷ 100, where Rate is the annual percentage rate and Time is in years.

What's the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal for the full term. Compound interest is calculated on principal plus previously earned interest, so it grows faster over time.

Where is simple interest commonly used?

Simple interest is common for short-term loans, certain bonds, and some basic savings calculations, where compounding isn't applied.

Simple Interest Explained

Simple interest is the most straightforward way to calculate interest on a loan or investment: it's based only on the original principal amount, and stays constant every year regardless of how much interest has already accumulated. This calculator computes simple interest instantly and shows the underlying formula so you can see exactly how the result was reached.

The Simple Interest Formula

Simple Interest = (Principal × Rate × Time) ÷ 100, where Principal is the initial amount, Rate is the annual interest rate as a percentage, and Time is the duration in years. The total amount payable (or the final value of an investment) is simply the principal plus this calculated interest.

Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

The key difference is what the interest is calculated on. Simple interest is always calculated on the original principal only, so the interest earned each year is identical. Compound interest, by contrast, is calculated on the principal plus all previously accumulated interest, so the amount of interest earned grows each period. Over short time periods or small amounts, the difference between the two is minor, but over longer periods, compound interest produces significantly more growth (or, for a loan, significantly more cost).

Where Simple Interest Is Used

  • Short-term personal loans that don't compound interest over their term.
  • Certain fixed-term bonds and certificates that pay a fixed simple interest rate.
  • Quick financial estimates where a straightforward, easy-to-verify calculation is preferred over compound growth modeling.
  • Educational contexts for teaching the basic concept of interest before introducing compounding.
